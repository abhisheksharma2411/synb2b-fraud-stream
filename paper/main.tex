\begin{abstract}
Fraud scores are not decisions. An accounts-payable team releases a payment, holds it
for dual-control approval, or routes it to an analyst, and the queue that receives it
has a fixed size. This work wraps a frozen scorer in a selective-decision layer that
controls the false-omission rate on auto-released traffic under a hard review budget.
The awkward part is that the policy chooses which labels it will ever see. Reviewed
items come back adjudicated within hours, released items are labelled only if a
dispute or a reconciliation exception surfaces, and held items are never labelled at
all. Calibration data therefore piles up in the review band, which sits at mid score,
while the quantity under control lives in the low-score tail. We derive a checkable
condition under which a target risk and a review budget are jointly attainable, and a
concentration bound on the exploration effort that keeps calibration valid. On a
100,000-invoice B2B stream carrying three injected drift events, an uncorrected
estimator overstates auto-release risk by a factor of 19.3 in steady state.
Inverse-arrival-propensity reweighting with a 0.120 exploration allocation moves the
ratio of truth to estimate up to 0.881 [0.834, 0.929], and quiet-regime risk lands at
0.012511 against a target of 0.0124. The exploration bound itself comes out vacuous,
and we report the measured rate in its place.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A fraud model returns a number. Nobody pays a number. Downstream of the score a
payment is released, parked for dual-control approval, or dropped into an analyst's
queue, and the size of that queue was fixed by headcount and an alert-queue SLA long
before anyone trained the model. Risk lands in that decision layer, which in most
shops is a pair of thresholds somebody moved last quarter and nobody has revisited
since.

Conformal prediction is the obvious tool for the job, since it turns a score into a
guarantee that holds in finite samples and asks almost nothing of the model that
produced it. Applying it here runs into a constraint the conformal literature rarely
carries: the abstention region has a hard capacity, because analysts cost money and a
payments organisation employs a countable number of them.

There is a second constraint, and it is the one this paper is about. The policy
chooses its own labels. An item sent to review comes back adjudicated within hours,
clean and correct. An item released on the auto-allow path is labelled only if
something surfaces, whether a chargeback representment, a duplicate-payment write-off
or a three-way match that fails at reconciliation, and on invoice fraud that takes 30
to 180 days when it happens at all. An item held for dual-control approval is never
labelled, because nobody ever learns what the payment would have done. So the
calibration set available at any moment is dominated by review-band items, and the
review band sits at mid score by construction, while the risk under control lives in
the low-score tail.

This is not staleness. It is bias, and filtering the calibration window by arrival
time does not repair it, because the items that never arrive are exactly the ones the
guarantee is about. Tighten the budget and calibration starves in the region that
needs it most, while the estimate of risk stays flat and every drift detector watching
the score distribution stays quiet.

Four things follow. A feasibility condition, stated as an equivalence under three
assumptions, that says when a target risk and a review budget can hold together at
all. A minimum-exploration argument in two parts, of which the identification half
survives and the rate half does not. A data extension, SynB2B-Fraud-Stream, that
annotates a published B2B invoice benchmark with three drift events and an
action-dependent arrival process, together with an empirical study spanning two
streams, six methods and five seeds. And a measurement of how wrong the naive
estimator gets, which is the number an operations director asks for first.

\section{Related Work}

Concurrent and independent work by Deng~\cite{deng2026} asks when fraud operations may
authorize automation at all, under shared analyst capacity, with finite-sample risk
control built on a KL divergence and an evaluation across three public fraud corpora.
The overlap is real and the framing is close. But that framework is not conformal, it
recalibrates nothing online, and it treats drift as a governance parameter somebody
declares rather than a process the policy has to track. It also treats label arrival
as exogenous, which is the assumption this paper spends its length attacking, and
while it does use randomized audit propensities, those exist to produce authorization
evidence; the randomization here exists to keep a calibration set identified, which is
a different job.

The calibration machinery is borrowed rather than invented. Split conformal prediction
and its inductive form supply the finite-sample statement~\cite{vovk2005,
papadopoulos2002}. Gibbs and Cand\`es replace the fixed quantile with an online
recursion that adapts under shift and later extend it to arbitrary
shifts~\cite{gibbs2021, gibbs2024}. Conformal risk control generalises coverage to
any monotone risk~\cite{angelopoulos2024}, with risk-controlling prediction sets as
the batch antecedent~\cite{bates2021}. Feldman and colleagues give the online version,
which is the recursion this policy runs~\cite{feldman2022}. Barber and colleagues
establish what survives when exchangeability fails, which on a card portfolio it does
within hours~\cite{barber2023}, and a PID formulation adds integral and derivative
terms to the same update~\cite{angelopoulos2023}.

Selective classification supplies the abstention vocabulary~\cite{geifman2017,
geifman2019} and learning to defer supplies the cost of routing to a
human~\cite{mozannar2020}. Neither caps how often the human may be asked.
Cost-sensitive conformal prediction with human-in-the-loop abstention trades review
cost against error in a soft objective over fifteen imbalanced batch
datasets~\cite{singh2026}, and Selective Conformal Risk Control certifies risk after
selection on exchangeable vision data, again with no budget~\cite{xu2026}.

Online shift detection with conformal adaptation is the nearest prior art to the audit
protocol here, and it monitors: no actions, no capacity constraint, no path from the
decision back into the data~\cite{leong2026}. Audited conformal prediction under
unknown shift~\cite{zhou2026} and anytime-valid conformal risk
control~\cite{hultberg2026} both tighten what a running system can certify. On the
fraud side the practitioner literature documented delayed and partial feedback a
decade ago~\cite{dalpozzolo2014, dalpozzolo2018}. Recent work formalises the limits
that delayed, censored and corrupted feedback impose in card
networks~\cite{dhama2026a}, studies causal label recovery in payment
graphs~\cite{dhama2026b}, and folds corrupted feedback into online conformal
prediction~\cite{wang2026}, while the selective-labels problem names the general
obstacle: a decision that suppresses an outcome also suppresses the evidence about
it~\cite{lakkaraju2017}.

\section{Problem Formulation}

Transactions arrive in time order. Transaction $t$ carries features $x_t$, an invoice
amount $v_t$ and a label $y_t \in \{0,1\}$ that may or may not ever be observed. A
scorer $s(\cdot)$, trained once and then frozen, emits $s_t = s(x_t)$. The policy
holds two thresholds $\tau^t_{\mathrm{lo}} \le \tau^t_{\mathrm{hi}}$ and maps the score
to an action,
\begin{equation}
a_t =
\begin{cases}
\textsf{allow} & s_t \le \tau^t_{\mathrm{lo}}\\
\textsf{hold} & s_t > \tau^t_{\mathrm{hi}}\\
\textsf{review} & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The controlled quantity is the false-omission rate on auto-released traffic,
$R_t = \Pr(y = 1 \mid a = \textsf{allow})$ over a trailing window, and the requirement
is $R_t \le \alpha$. Review capacity is a hard cap: the share of traffic reaching an
analyst may not exceed $b$. Held volume is capped at $\beta$, which in accounts payable
is a working-capital limit rather than a risk limit, since a hold delays a supplier
payment and delayed suppliers stop shipping.

The filtration is where this departs from the usual setup. Write $A_i$ for the arrival
time of transaction $i$'s label. The calibration set at time $t$ is
$\mathcal{C}_t = \{ i < t : A_i \le t \}$, and $A_i$ depends on $a_i$, which the
policy chose. So $\mathcal{C}_t$ is not a sample of the past. It is the past, filtered
through the policy's own decisions, and the filter is tightest exactly where the
guarantee needs data.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Arrival model}

Three channels, one per action. A reviewed item is adjudicated after
$\mathrm{Gamma}(2, 0.4)$ days and its label is the truth. A released item discloses
with probability $p_d(x)$, and if it does the label arrives after
$\min(\mathrm{Gamma}(2, 30), 180)$ days; if it does not, the ledger closes the item at
180 days and books it clean whatever it really was. That second half matters more than
the delay. An undisclosed fraud is not a missing row in an accounts-payable ledger, it
is a row marked good, and any estimator that treats absence as missingness will read
the wrong sign off it. A held item is never labelled.

\subsection{Inverse-arrival-propensity reweighting}

Let $\pi_i$ be the probability that transaction $i$'s label has arrived by the current
time. On the review path $\pi_i$ is known by construction, because the policy chose the
review and the SLA is an operational fact. On the release path
$\pi_i = p_d(x_i)\,F_G(t - t_i)$ with $F_G$ the disclosure delay law, and $p_d$ has to
be estimated, which is where the modelling risk sits. Each calibration item enters the
risk estimate with weight $\rho^{\,t - t_i} / \pi_i$, geometric decay over a window
capped in both days and count. Items with $\pi_i$ below a floor are dropped from
numerator and denominator alike rather than clipped. A decision taken nine days ago has
had almost no chance to report, its inverse propensity is enormous, and nearly all of
what it contributes is variance.

\subsection{Update, budget projection, infeasibility}

The recursion is the Gibbs--Cand\`es one, driven by the lagged and reweighted label
stream,
\begin{equation}
\alpha_{t+1} = \alpha_t + \gamma\,(\alpha - \mathrm{err}_t),
\end{equation}
with $\tau_{\mathrm{lo}}$ read off the reweighted risk curve, either as a quantile
(M3) or through a conformal-risk-control threshold search over the same window (M4,
M5). A candidate threshold counts only if at least $200$ units of calibration weight
sit at or below it. Certifying a one-in-a-hundred risk from a dozen observations is
not conservatism, it is small arithmetic.

Capacity is enforced separately. Given $\tau_{\mathrm{lo}}$, the upper threshold
$\tau_{\mathrm{hi}}$ is placed by an online quantile so the band carries exactly
$b(1-\varepsilon)$ of traffic, which decouples the risk constraint from the capacity
constraint and stops the two fighting over one knob. When no admissible
$\tau_{\mathrm{lo}}$ meets $\alpha$ within the available capacity, the policy widens
the band as far as $b$ allows and then raises a \emph{budget-infeasible} flag for that
window. It does not quietly miss the target and report success. That flag is a
deliverable of the method, not an error condition, and Section~\ref{sec:results}
reports how often it fires.

\subsection{Exploration}

A fraction $\varepsilon$ of review capacity is spent on transactions drawn uniformly
at random from below $\tau_{\mathrm{lo}}$, independent of score. These are items the
policy is already confident about. Spending analyst time on them looks like waste on
any dashboard an operations manager reads, and that is precisely why it gets cut first
in production, which is the practical failure this paper is trying to name. Their
labels are the only ones drawn from the auto-allow region with a known probability, so
they are the only ones that can test the extrapolation the estimator would otherwise
be making.

\subsection{When are the target and the budget compatible?}

Let $F$ be the score law, $r(u) = \Pr(y=1 \mid s = u)$ the conditional fraud rate and
$R(\tau) = \mathbb{E}[y \mid s \le \tau]$ the risk on everything released below
$\tau$. The argument needs three assumptions, and one is doing real work. (A1) $F$ is continuous and strictly
increasing on the operating range. (A2) $r$ is non-decreasing in the score. (A3) the
analyst queue is standing capacity and is always spent, so the band carries
$b(1-\varepsilon)$.

\begin{proposition}[Feasibility]
\label{prop:feas}
Under \emph{(A1)--(A3)}, write $q = 1 - \beta - b(1-\varepsilon)$. The pair
$(\alpha, b)$ is jointly attainable if and only if
$R\!\left(F^{-1}(q)\right) \le \alpha$; equivalently
$\alpha \ge \alpha_{\min}(b) := R(F^{-1}(1 - \beta - b(1-\varepsilon)))$, and
$\alpha_{\min}$ is non-increasing in $b$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Under (A3) the held mass is $1 - F(\tau_{\mathrm{lo}}) - b(1-\varepsilon)$, so the cap
$\beta$ forces $F(\tau_{\mathrm{lo}}) \ge q$. Under (A2) the map $R$ is
non-decreasing: for $\tau_1 < \tau_2$, $R(\tau_2)$ is a convex combination of
$R(\tau_1)$ and $\mathbb{E}[y \mid \tau_1 < s \le \tau_2]$, and the second term is at
least $r(\tau_1) \ge R(\tau_1)$. The smallest admissible allow set is therefore the
one at $F^{-1}(q)$, and it is feasible exactly when its risk clears $\alpha$.
Monotonicity in $b$ follows because $q$ decreases as $b$ grows.
\end{proof}

Drop (A2) and the ``only if'' survives while the ``if'' does not, because the optimal
release set need no longer be an interval in the score. The implementation raises its
flag on the \emph{estimated} $R$, so the guarantee is worth exactly what the estimator
is worth, which is what the next proposition is about.

\begin{proposition}[Identification]
\label{prop:ident}
On the release path only $Z = Y \cdot D$ is observed, with
$D \sim \mathrm{Bern}(p_d(x))$ independent of $Y$ given $x$. Then
$\Pr(Z = 1 \mid x) = r(x)\,p_d(x)$, so for any $c(x)$ with
$p_d(x) \le c(x) \le 1/r(x)$ the pair $(cr,\; p_d/c)$ is again a rate-probability
pair and induces the same observable law. Only the product is identified from the
ledger channel.
\end{proposition}

The review band identifies $r$ on $(\tau_{\mathrm{lo}}, \tau_{\mathrm{hi}}]$, which is
disjoint from the release region, so it constrains $r$ there only through a
functional-form assumption. An exploration sample drawn from below
$\tau_{\mathrm{lo}}$ with known probability identifies $r$ directly, and
$p_d = \Pr(Z{=}1 \mid x)/r(x)$ follows. The honest reading is therefore weaker than
the one we set out to prove. At $\varepsilon = 0$ the release-path risk is not
unidentified; it is identified only through an extrapolation that no data in the
release region can test.

We then asked how much exploration a rate guarantee needs. Bernstein's inequality on
the exploration sample, with $R \approx \alpha$, gives
$|\hat{R} - R| \le \eta\alpha$ with probability $1-\delta$ once
$n_{\exp} \ge 2\left[(1-\alpha) + \eta/3\right]\log(2/\delta) / (\eta^2 \alpha)$,
and since
$n_{\exp} \approx \varepsilon b L$ over a window of length $L$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:eps}
\varepsilon \;\ge\; \frac{2\left[(1-\alpha) + \eta/3\right]\log(2/\delta)}
{\eta^{2}\,\alpha\,b\,L}.
\end{equation}
At the operating point of Section~\ref{sec:setup} this asks for
$n_{\exp} \ge 786$, that is $\varepsilon \ge 0.873$, merely to pin the estimate within
one target-width. Halve the tolerance and it demands 2748 explored items, an
$\varepsilon$ of 3.05, three times the whole budget. The bound is vacuous here.
We report the measured rate in its place and say so again in the results.

\subsection{Audit protocol}

Every window emits four numbers: the reweighted risk estimate, the realised risk over
matured labels, the review demand against $b$, and the infeasibility flag. The ratio
of the last two pairs is what an auditor should read. A policy whose estimate is flat
while its realised risk climbs is not stable, it is blind, and no detector watching
only the score distribution will say so.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Budgeted conformal decisioning with exploration}
\label{alg:main}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{input} target $\alpha$, budget $b$, hold cap $\beta$, decay $\rho$,
step $\gamma$, exploration share $\varepsilon$
\STATE $\alpha_1 \leftarrow \alpha$; $\mathcal{C} \leftarrow \emptyset$
\FOR{each arriving transaction $t$}
  \STATE $s_t \leftarrow s(x_t)$
  \STATE admit to $\mathcal{C}$ every label with $A_i \le t$ \COMMENT{causal guard}
  \IF{$t \bmod m = 0$}
    \STATE $w_i \leftarrow \rho^{\,t-t_i}/\pi_i$ for $i \in \mathcal{C}$, trimming
      $\pi_i < \pi_0$
    \STATE $\hat{R}(\cdot) \leftarrow$ weighted risk curve with support gate
    \STATE $\tau_{\mathrm{lo}} \leftarrow \max\{\tau : \hat{R}(\tau) \le \alpha_t\}$
    \IF{no such $\tau$ within $\beta$}
      \STATE widen to the cap and raise \textsf{infeasible}
    \ENDIF
    \STATE $\tau_{\mathrm{hi}} \leftarrow$ online quantile giving band mass
      $b(1-\varepsilon)$
    \STATE $\alpha_{t+1} \leftarrow \alpha_t + \gamma(\alpha - \mathrm{err}_t)$
  \ENDIF
  \STATE $a_t \leftarrow$ action from $(s_t, \tau_{\mathrm{lo}}, \tau_{\mathrm{hi}})$
  \IF{$a_t = \textsf{allow}$ and $u_t < \varepsilon b / F(\tau_{\mathrm{lo}})$}
    \STATE $a_t \leftarrow \textsf{review}$ \COMMENT{exploration draw}
  \ENDIF
  \STATE schedule $A_t$ from the channel implied by $a_t$
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

The primary stream is SynB2B-Fraud~\cite{synb2b}, a published benchmark of 100,000
buyer-to-supplier invoices over 561.34 days with five fraud topologies. Its statistics
were re-derived from the CSV rather than copied from the datasheet: 1,500 fraudulent
rows, exactly 300 per topology, a median invoice of 3,492.66. Fraud is not spread
evenly in time in that benchmark, and the first 20\% of rows by count carries 0.385\%
fraud against 1.78\% over the rest, so a target calibrated on the training split would
mean nothing. Targets are stipulated instead, at $\alpha = 0.0124$ here and
$\alpha = 0.000930$ on the card stream, chosen to be reachable in the quiet regime and
out of reach during two of the three drift events.

We add two annotation layers, released as SynB2B-Fraud-Stream. Drift enters as a
second fraud channel layered on the generator's own labels, never as a relabelling, so
all 1,500 published labels survive: a covariate shift in the log-amount law, a 60-day
sign flip of the supplier-novelty coefficient, and a base-rate surge with a 9-day
half-life. Magnitudes are solved against stated targets rather than picked by eye.
Arrival follows Section~\ref{sec:method}'s channels with disclosure probabilities
running from 0.86 for wire redirection down to 0.19 for payment-term manipulation,
because a redirected wire generates a phone call and a manipulated payment term
generates nothing.

The scorer is LightGBM, trained on the first 20\% in time order and then frozen, which
isolates the calibration layer from model staleness. It reaches AUC-PR 0.2049 and
AUC-ROC 0.7313 over the remaining 80\%. Relative AUC-PR over the ten days after each
event falls by 0.7988 and 0.6805 for the covariate and concept shifts, and
\emph{rises} by 0.3233 after the prior shift, since a larger positive class makes
average precision easier. External validity comes from the ULB card
portfolio~\cite{dalpozzolo2018}, replayed in its original row order on the same
calendar. Cost constants are stipulated per stream and stated in the repository, and the
claim made here is risk control, not cost optimality. Everything runs five seeds with
10,000-resample percentile intervals, on an Apple M4 Pro. Code, the full results file
and the decision log are at
\ifanon URL withheld for review\else \url{https://github.com/abhisheksharma2411/synb2b-fraud-stream}\fi.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Table~\ref{tab:methods} holds the comparison. M0 and M1 coincide exactly, to every
digit, because the training split's risk curve lies wholly below the target and the
finite-sample correction has nothing to bite on. A conformal quantile computed once on
an unrepresentative split is not different from a threshold somebody eyeballed, which
is worth saying out loud before any adaptive method is credited with beating it. Both
leave quiet-regime risk at 0.017436 [0.017196, 0.017836], which overshoots
$\alpha$ by 40.6\%. M5 lands at 0.012511 [0.012189, 0.012854], over target by 0.90\%.

The estimator numbers are the ones to read next. Over matured windows M4 recovers a
ratio of truth to estimate of 0.0518, meaning it reads its own risk high by a factor
of 19.3, and it does so while its realised risk tracks the target well enough to look
healthy on a dashboard. M3 sits at 0.0844 and cost-sensitive M2 at 0.112. Adding
inverse-arrival-propensity weights and exploration moves M5 to 0.881 [0.834, 0.929],
an overstatement of 13.5\%, and handing the true disclosure propensity to the same
policy gives 1.07, which brackets the remaining error as propensity-model error rather
than anything structural. Fig.~\ref{fig:comp} shows why the correction is needed at
all: by the end of the stream 0.9816 of calibration weight arrives through the ledger
channel, 0.0158 through the review band and 0.0026 through exploration.

Capacity holds. M5 draws review demand of 0.02269 against a budget of 0.0200, a mean
overshoot of 0.002787, with 0.44 of windows above budget before projection and a
served rate of 0.01806 after it. Cost per thousand transactions is 172993 for M5
against 161420 for M2, so the proposed method is the most expensive of the adaptive
ones. It buys an estimator that can be believed, not a cheaper queue. Fraud value
released is 18,483,140 [18,189,283, 18,776,997] of 30,002,619 total, against
24,417,614 under the static rule. The calibration update costs 18.36 [17.99, 18.59]
microseconds at p50 and 26.44 [25.47, 27.34] at p99 per transaction, amortised over
batches of 250, which puts it far below the network hop it rides on.

\eqref{eq:eps} never comes close to describing: at 0.400 that bound is still
unsatisfied by a wide margin while the estimator sits within 0.063 of unbiased.
Conservative bounds are conservative. Neither proposition was rewritten to
match a measurement, and the gap is stated rather than closed.

One ablation came out backwards, and it is the most useful thing in the study. Set the
delay multiplier to 0 so every label arrives the instant the decision is taken, and the
infeasibility flag fires in 1.0000 of windows, against 0.341 at the true delay, while
realised risk improves to 0.019487. That is not perfect feedback, because the
undisclosed frauds are still booked clean at the ledger close; it is the delay alone,
removed. And removing it made the policy louder, not calmer. The lag was never
protecting anything, it was concealing the fact that the
target could not be met at this capacity, and the delayed estimator was quiet because
it was wrong. Raising disclosure has a related effect. At a multiplier of 2.4, so that
released frauds surface far more often, the corrected estimator's bias ratio degrades
from 0.881 to 0.669 while infeasibility climbs to 0.846, because more disclosure
sharpens the ledger channel without touching the censoring that sits underneath it.

Misses split by topology exactly as disclosure does. Wire redirection surfaces 0.86 of
the time and is missed at 0.0902 [0.0640, 0.1286]. Payment-term manipulation surfaces
at 0.19 and is missed at 0.649. Shell-supplier rings sit at 0.870 and drift-induced
fraud at 0.948, so the policy is blindest precisely where the ledger is quietest, and a
reader who cares about invoice fraud rather than about conformal prediction should take
that as the operational finding. Recovery is uneven too: 18.32 days after the covariate
shift for every method, and after the concept and prior events realised risk never
returns to target before the stream ends. Recovery never came.

\section{Discussion and Threats to Validity}

The infeasibility rate is a result, not a defect. M5 declares 0.341 of windows
infeasible, M2 declares 0.924, and M0 declares none while missing the target by more
than any of them. A policy that never reports infeasibility under a capacity
constraint and a drifting stream is not succeeding; it is not looking. Operations
teams should demand that number on the same screen as the risk estimate.

Practitioners treat label lag as an engineering nuisance to be absorbed by a longer
window. That reading is wrong. A longer window admits more matured labels and more
censored ones in the same motion, and the censored ones are not noise, they carry a
sign.

The limits below deserve to be stated plainly rather than softened. The drift in this
study was injected by us, with magnitudes we solved against targets we chose, so every
recovery-time number here describes our generator and not any real payment network,
and bootstrap intervals do nothing about that. The cost constants are stipulated,
which is why the cost column is reported and never argued from. And the propositions
lean on assumptions (A1)--(A3), of which the monotone-conditional-rate assumption is
the one a real portfolio breaks first, whenever a topology concentrates at mid score.

What would break this in production is narrower than any of that: the disclosure model
is fitted on the same censored ledger it is meant to correct, so if an operations team
changes its write-off policy or its wire recall window, $p_d$ moves with no signal at
all in the score distribution. The audit protocol catches that only after a full
reconciliation horizon has elapsed. Six months is a long time to be wrong.

\section{Conclusion}

A budgeted fraud policy decides which labels it will ever receive, and that makes its
own calibration set a function of its own actions. We gave a feasibility condition
that says when a target and a budget can coexist, and an identification result that
says what random exploration buys. The rate bound attached to it turned out vacuous at
any operating point worth running, so the measured curve stands in for it. What holds
up across two streams is narrower and more useful than a theorem: correcting for
arrival propensity moves an estimator that reads high by a factor of 19.3 to one that
reads high by 13.5\%, and paying for that correction costs 0.120 of the analyst
budget and roughly nine microseconds per transaction.
